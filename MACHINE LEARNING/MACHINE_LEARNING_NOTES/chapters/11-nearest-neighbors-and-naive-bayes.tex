\chapter{Nearest Neighbors and Naive Bayes}
\label{ch:knn-nb}

\chapterguide%
  {Distance measures from \chapref{ch:linear-algebra}, Bayes' theorem from \chapref{ch:statistics}, and model-selection ideas from \chapref{ch:training}.}
  {Run $k$-nearest neighbors by hand, choose $k$ and a distance sensibly, explain why naive Bayes multiplies per-feature probabilities, and apply Laplace smoothing to a small text example.}

This chapter pairs two refreshingly simple classifiers. $k$-nearest neighbors predicts from raw similarity: ``you are probably like the examples closest to you.'' Naive Bayes predicts from probability bookkeeping: ``which class makes these feature values least surprising?'' Both can be computed by hand, both make strong baselines, and both teach lessons that echo through the rest of machine learning.

\begin{firstread}
Think of two human strategies. Nearest neighbors asks, ``what happened to the most similar past cases?'' Naive Bayes asks, ``which possible answer makes the evidence I see most believable?'' Learn those questions first; the distance and probability calculations merely make the questions repeatable.
\end{firstread}

\section{$k$-nearest neighbors}
\label{sec:knn}

$k$-NN has no training phase worth the name: it simply \emph{stores} the training data. All the work happens at prediction time:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item compute the distance from the new observation to every stored training observation;
  \item keep the $k$ closest ones --- the \term{neighbors};
  \item for classification, predict the majority class among the neighbors; for regression, predict the average of their labels.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{workedexample}
Training data: $A=(1,1)$ and $B=(2,1)$ labeled Red; $C=(4,4)$ and $D=(5,4)$ labeled Blue. Classify the new point $(2,2)$ with $k=3$ using Euclidean distance:
\begin{gather*}
  d_A=\sqrt{1^2+1^2}\approx 1.41,\qquad
  d_B=\sqrt{0^2+1^2}=1.00,\\
  d_C=\sqrt{2^2+2^2}\approx 2.83,\qquad
  d_D=\sqrt{3^2+2^2}\approx 3.61.
\end{gather*}
The three nearest are $B$ (Red), $A$ (Red), and $C$ (Blue). The vote is Red 2, Blue 1, so the prediction is Red.
\end{workedexample}

\subsection{Choosing $k$}

The single hyperparameter $k$ is a smoothness dial:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \term{Small $k$} (say $k=1$) yields a jagged, flexible boundary that can capture fine structure but also copies every mislabeled or noisy training point --- low bias, high variance.
  \item \term{Large $k$} averages over a wide neighborhood, producing a smooth, stable boundary that may blur real distinctions; at the extreme $k=n$, the model always predicts the overall majority class --- high bias, low variance.
\end{itemize}

Choose $k$ with validation data or cross-validation, use an odd $k$ in binary problems to avoid tied votes, and consider \term{distance-weighted voting}, in which each neighbor's vote is weighted by $1/d$ so nearer neighbors count more.

$k$-NN also does regression --- predict the average (or distance-weighted average) label of the neighbors --- which yields a locally adaptive, assumption-free fit, at the price of the same sensitivities listed above.

\begin{intuition}
$k$-NN never builds a theory of the data; it just answers every question with ``let me check what similar past cases did.'' That makes it honest and flexible --- and completely dependent on the distance actually measuring similarity that matters.
\end{intuition}

\begin{intuition}
The lone orange point sitting up in the blue region is probably noise. With $k=1$ the boundary detours around it, carving out a little island --- the model has memorized a mistake. \end{intuition}

\section{Naive Bayes}
\label{sec:naive-bayes}

Naive Bayes turns classification into the question Bayes' theorem was built for: given the observed features, which class is most probable?
\[
  P(y\given x_1,\ldots,x_p)
  \;\propto\;
  P(y)\prod_{j=1}^{p}P(x_j\given y).
\]
Decoding the formula: $P(y)$ is the \term{prior} --- how common the class is before looking at any features; each $P(x_j\given y)$ is the \term{likelihood} of one feature value within that class; and the product is where the ``naive'' assumption lives --- the features are treated as conditionally independent given the class (\chapref{ch:statistics}). That assumption is often false (words in an email are not independent), yet the resulting classifier can still rank classes well in practice.

Prediction picks the class with the largest score, $\hat{y}=\argmax_y P(y)\prod_j P(x_j\given y)$, and in software the product is computed as a sum of logarithms so that multiplying hundreds of small probabilities does not underflow to zero.

\subsection{Three standard variants}

\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\bfseries\leavevmode\color{MLNavy}}p{0.20\linewidth}p{0.25\linewidth}X@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{MLPaleBlue!92}
Variant & Feature type & How it models the features \\
\midrule
Gaussian NB & Continuous features & Each $P(x_j\given y)$ is a normal density with the class-specific mean and variance of feature $j$. \\
Multinomial NB & Count data, such as word counts & $P(x_j\given y)$ is the probability of word $j$ in class $y$'s text, and repeated words count repeatedly. It is the workhorse for text classification. \\
Bernoulli NB & Binary presence/absence features & Explicitly models both a word appearing and a word being absent. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{mltable}

\subsection{Laplace smoothing}

Estimating $P(\text{word}\given\text{class})$ by raw frequency has a fatal flaw: any word never seen in a class's training text gets probability zero, and one zero annihilates the whole product. \term{Laplace (add-one) smoothing} pretends every word was seen once more than it was:
\[
  P(w\given y)
  =\frac{\operatorname{count}(w,y)+1}
        {\operatorname{total\ words\ in\ }y+V},
\]
where $V$ is the vocabulary size. No probability is ever exactly zero, and the estimates barely change for well-observed words.

\begin{workedexample}
A tiny spam filter with vocabulary $\{$money, free, meeting, lunch$\}$ ($V=4$) is trained on 10 words of spam and 10 words of ham, with equal priors $P(\text{spam})=P(\text{ham})=0.5$:

\begin{mltable}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{MLPaleBlue!92}
raw counts & money & free & meeting & lunch \\
\midrule
spam (10 words) & 5 & 4 & 1 & 0 \\
ham (10 words) & 1 & 1 & 5 & 3 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{mltable}

With smoothing, every likelihood is $(\text{count}+1)/(10+4)$; for example $P(\text{money}\given\text{spam})=6/14$ and $P(\text{money}\given\text{ham})=2/14$. Classify the message ``free money'':
\[
  \text{spam score}=0.5\times\tfrac{6}{14}\times\tfrac{5}{14}
  =\tfrac{15}{196},
  \qquad
  \text{ham score}=0.5\times\tfrac{2}{14}\times\tfrac{2}{14}
  =\tfrac{2}{196}.
\]
Normalizing, $P(\text{spam}\given\text{message})=15/(15+2)\approx 0.88$: the message is classified as spam. Note that without smoothing, a message containing ``lunch'' could never be spam, no matter what else it said.
\end{workedexample}

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Training is a single counting pass, prediction is instant, and the model scales effortlessly to millions of features --- which is why it remains a standard first model for text.
  \item It performs surprisingly well with little training data, because it estimates only per-feature statistics rather than feature interactions.
  \item Its predicted probabilities are often \emph{overconfident} (the independence assumption can double-count correlated evidence), so validate the probability values and recalibrate them when decisions require calibrated risk (\chapref{ch:evaluation}).
  \item It cannot represent interactions: if a pattern only exists in a \emph{combination} of features (``free'' and ``money'' together, but neither alone), naive Bayes cannot learn it.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keyidea}
$k$-NN and naive Bayes make opposite simplifications. $k$-NN keeps the training examples rather than estimating a fixed parametric form, but it still assumes that the chosen distance and local neighborhood are meaningful. Naive Bayes compresses the data into per-class, per-feature summaries and assumes conditional independence. Both are quick to build, valuable as baselines, and clear about what they ignore.
\end{keyidea}
