\chapref{ch:classification} ended with an open question: when many straight lines separate the classes, which one should a classifier choose? The support vector machine (SVM) gives an influential answer: choose the line with the \emph{widest margin} --- the one that stays as far as possible from the nearest points of both classes. Points on or inside that margin are the \term{support vectors}, and they determine the boundary.

\begin{firstread}
Core path: keep the margin picture, then learn soft margins, $C$, kernels, and $\gamma$. The practical goal is to know when an SVM is a sensible comparison model and why scaling matters.
\end{firstread}

\begin{intuition}
Imagine the boundary as the center line of a street between two crowds. The SVM builds the \emph{widest possible street} that touches neither crowd; the people leaning against the curbs are the support vectors. Everyone standing far back could go home --- removing them would not move the street at all.
\end{intuition}

\section{The hard-margin objective}

With labels $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, a linear SVM scores points with $\bm{w}^{\mathsf T}\bm{x}+b$ and requires every training point to sit on its correct side with room to spare:
\[
  \min_{\bm{w},b}\ \frac{1}{2}\|\bm{w}\|_2^2
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad
  y_i(\bm{w}^{\mathsf T}\bm{x}_i+b)\geq 1 .
\]
Decoding the pieces: the constraint $y_i(\bm{w}^{\mathsf T}\bm{x}_i+b)\geq 1$ says ``the score has the right sign, and its size is at least 1'' --- points exactly at 1 lie on the dashed margin lines. Geometrically, the distance between the two dashed lines works out to $2/\|\bm{w}\|_2$, so \emph{minimizing} $\|\bm{w}\|$ is exactly \emph{maximizing} the margin; squaring and halving just make the calculus tidy.

\section{The soft margin: allowing exceptions}

Real classes overlap, so the SVM used in practice permits violations and charges for them:
\[
  \min_{\bm{w},b}\
  \frac{1}{2}\|\bm{w}\|_2^2
  +C\sum_{i=1}^{n}
  \max\!\left(0,\,1-y_i(\bm{w}^{\mathsf T}\bm{x}_i+b)\right).
\]
The second term is the \term{hinge loss}: zero for points comfortably beyond the margin, growing linearly for points inside it or on the wrong side. The hyperparameter $C$ sets the exchange rate between a wide street and few violations:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \term{Large $C$}: violations are expensive, so the boundary contorts to classify nearly everything correctly --- narrow margin, higher risk of overfitting.
  \item \term{Small $C$}: violations are cheap, so the model keeps a wide, calm margin and tolerates some training errors --- stronger regularization.
\end{itemize}

Written as ``hinge loss plus an $L_2$ penalty,'' the soft-margin SVM lines up neatly beside regularized logistic regression, which is ``cross-entropy loss plus an $L_2$ penalty'': same skeleton, different loss. The hinge loss stops caring once a point clears the margin, which is why only the support vectors matter; cross-entropy keeps rewarding extra confidence forever, which is why logistic regression uses every point.

\section{Curved boundaries: mapping up}

Some datasets cannot be separated by any straight line --- but they can be after the features are transformed.

\begin{workedexample}
On a number line, let $x\in\{-2,2\}$ be class A and $x\in\{-1,0,1\}$ be class B. No single threshold on $x$ separates A from B (A sits on \emph{both} sides). Now map each point upward to two features, $\phi(x)=(x,\,x^2)$:
\begin{mltable}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{MLPaleBlue!92}
$x$ & $-2$ & $-1$ & $0$ & $1$ & $2$ \\
\midrule
$x^2$ & $4$ & $1$ & $0$ & $1$ & $4$ \\
class & A & B & B & B & A \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{mltable}
In the new $(x,x^2)$ plane, class A floats at height 4 and class B sits at height $\leq 1$: the horizontal line $x^2=2.5$ separates them perfectly. A linear boundary in the mapped space is a \emph{curved} boundary (here, $|x|=\sqrt{2.5}$) in the original space.
\end{workedexample}

\section{The kernel trick}

Explicitly building rich feature maps gets expensive fast --- and, for the most useful maps, impossible (they are infinite-dimensional). The escape hatch is that the SVM's training and prediction equations can be rearranged to use the data \emph{only through inner products} $\phi(\bm{x})^{\mathsf T}\phi(\bm{x}')$. A \term{kernel function} computes that inner product directly from the original inputs,
\[
  k(\bm{x},\bm{x}')=\phi(\bm{x})^{\mathsf T}\phi(\bm{x}'),
\]
so the mapped features never need to be constructed at all --- this shortcut is the \term{kernel trick}. Standard choices:

\begin{mltable}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{MLPaleBlue!92}
Kernel & Formula & Boundary it produces \\
\midrule
Linear & $\bm{x}^{\mathsf T}\bm{x}'$ & Straight; fast on huge, sparse data \\
Polynomial & ${(\gamma\,\bm{x}^{\mathsf T}\bm{x}'+r)}^d$ & Curved, degree-$d$ interactions \\
RBF (Gaussian) & $\exp\!\left(-\gamma\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}'\|_2^2\right)$ & Smooth, local, very flexible \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{mltable}

The \term{RBF kernel} is the default: it reads as ``similarity that decays with distance,'' and its $\gamma$ sets how fast. Small $\gamma$ means every support vector influences a wide area (smooth boundary, may underfit); large $\gamma$ means influence is a tight bubble around each point (wiggly boundary, may overfit). In practice, $C$ and $\gamma$ are tuned \emph{together} on a logarithmic grid --- they interact strongly.

\begin{intuition}
An RBF-kernel SVM predicts like a panel of landmarks: each support vector shouts its own class with a volume that fades with distance ($\gamma$ is how fast the shout fades), and a new point goes to whichever side shouts louder where it stands. The kernel trick is the accounting identity that lets this infinitely-rich comparison be computed with one cheap formula per pair of points.
\end{intuition}

\begin{keyidea}
A kernel does not ``bend'' a trained straight line afterward. It changes the similarity representation used while fitting, which lets a linear separator in that representation correspond to a curved boundary in the original feature space.

\begin{analogy}
$C$ is a strictness setting for a teacher marking homework. A low $C$ teacher accepts that a few students will get things wrong and teaches a general rule. A high $C$ teacher insists that every single student's answer be accommodated, and ends up teaching a rule so contorted that nobody can apply it to a new question.

$\gamma$ is the radius of each training point's voice. Low $\gamma$ means every point is heard across the whole room, producing a smooth global boundary. High $\gamma$ means each point is only heard by its immediate neighbors, producing islands around individual examples.
\end{analogy}

\section{Multiclass SVMs and support vector regression}

The SVM is natively binary. Multiclass problems are handled by reduction: \term{one-vs-rest} trains $K$ machines (each class against all others), while \term{one-vs-one} trains a machine for every pair of classes and lets them vote; both work well.

\term{Support vector regression (SVR)} recycles the margin idea for numeric labels: build a tube of half-width $\varepsilon$ around the prediction and charge nothing for points inside it, using the $\varepsilon$-insensitive loss $\max(0,\,|y-f(\bm{x})|-\varepsilon)$. Only points on or outside the tube become support vectors, giving a sparse, outlier-tolerant regressor with the full kernel toolbox available.

\section{Practical notes}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \term{Scale the features.} Apply the distance-geometry rule from \secref{sec:scaling}.
  \item \term{Tune $C$ (and $\gamma$)} by cross-validation over logarithmic grids such as $10^{-2},\ldots,10^{3}$.
  \item \term{Know the sweet spot:} SVMs shine on small-to-medium datasets, high-dimensional problems, and text (linear kernel); training scales poorly to hundreds of thousands of observations, where tree ensembles or linear models are more practical.
  \item \term{Probabilities are not native.} SVMs output scores and margins; if calibrated probabilities are needed, apply and validate a calibration step (\chapref{ch:evaluation}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Choosing a supervised model}
\label{sec:supervised-model-choice}

Stage 3B ends here, so the supervised model-family menu can now be laid on the table.

\begin{mltable}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.19\linewidth}p{0.36\linewidth}p{0.36\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{MLPaleBlue!92}
Model & Useful strengths & Important limitations \\
\midrule
Linear regression & Interpretable, fast, the standard first baseline & Linear relationships only; sensitive to outliers \\
Ridge / lasso & Controls overfitting; lasso selects features & Still linear; needs feature scaling \\
Logistic regression & Strong probability baseline, convex training, interpretable odds & Linear boundary; calibration still needs checking \\
LDA / QDA & Fast, stable on small data & Gaussian class assumptions \\
$k$-NN & No training, flexible boundaries & Slow at prediction; scale- and dimension-sensitive \\
Naive Bayes & Very fast, strong text baseline & Independence and calibration caveats (\secref{sec:naive-bayes}) \\
Decision tree & Readable rules, no scaling, mixed data & Unstable; overfits without pruning \\
Random forest & Robust, accurate, minimal tuning & Larger, slower, less transparent \\
Gradient boosting & Often a very strong tabular benchmark & Needs careful tuning and early stopping \\
SVM & Margins and kernels; strong in high dimensions & Scaling required; slow on very large data \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{mltable}

A sensible default path: start with a regularized linear model as the baseline; try a random forest for a near-free accuracy check; reach for tuned gradient boosting when accuracy matters most; and consider an SVM when the data is high-dimensional or modest in size. Let validation results --- not fashion --- make the final call.

\begin{keyidea}
The SVM contributes two permanent ideas to machine learning. First, among all boundaries that fit, prefer the one with the largest safety margin --- a built-in form of regularization. Second, a linear method plus the kernel trick becomes a nonlinear method at almost no conceptual cost. Margins and kernels outlived the SVM's peak popularity and now echo throughout the field.
\end{keyidea}
