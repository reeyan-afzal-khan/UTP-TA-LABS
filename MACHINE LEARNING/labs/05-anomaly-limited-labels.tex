\labsection{5}{Unsupervised learning II: anomaly detection and limited labels}{sec:lab05-anomaly-limited-labels}

\begin{labproject}{Ranking card fraud without labels, and getting the most from a small labeling budget}
\textbf{Read first.} Chapters~\ref{ch:anomaly} and~\ref{ch:semi-supervised}.\\
\textbf{Goal.} Rank card transactions for review with an anomaly detector that never sees a fraud label, judge the ranking with metrics that respect a 0.17\% base rate, then stretch a small labeling budget with self-training and active learning, checking each against a labels-only baseline.\\
\textbf{Scenario.} A bank's fraud team can review a few hundred alerts a day and wants the right ones at the top of the list. A bank's support team wants customer messages routed to the right one of 77 desks automatically, and can pay for only a thousand or so of them to be labeled by hand. In both cases labels are the scarce resource, and the question is how far you can get without them.\\
\textbf{Data.} In \texttt{all\_datasets/}: \texttt{unsupervised/\allowbreak creditcard\_fraud\_ulb/\allowbreak creditcard\_fraud\_ulb.zip}, 284,807 transactions by European cardholders over two days in September 2013, released by the Machine Learning Group of the Universit\'e Libre de Bruxelles (Dal Pozzolo and others, 2015); and \texttt{advanced/\allowbreak banking77/\allowbreak banking77.csv}, 13,083 messages to a bank's support chat, each labeled with one of 77 intents (Casanueva and others, PolyAI, 2020).\\
\textbf{Notebook.} \texttt{all\_experiments/\allowbreak lab05\_anomaly\_limited\_labels/\allowbreak lab05\_anomaly\_limited\_labels\_worked.ipynb}, with a README beside it.\\
\textbf{Hand in.} The executed notebook, \texttt{results/lab05/} and a short conclusion, as listed in the checklist at the end of this sheet.
\end{labproject}

\begin{labquestion}{5}{Split the messages three ways}
Banking77 holds 13,083 messages that customers typed to a bank's support chat, each labeled by hand with one of 77 intents, such as \texttt{card\_arrival} or \texttt{exchange\_rate}. Labels cost money, so this part pretends most are unknown: a pool of 8,003 messages to learn from, 2,000 for validation, and the official 3,080 test messages, opened once. Turning text into numbers needs only the words, so the TF-IDF vocabulary of single words and word pairs is learned from the pool without any labels.
\end{labquestion}

\end{labanswer}

\begin{labquestion}{6}{Self-train from 1,000 labels}
Keep the labels of 1,000 pool messages, an eighth of the pool and about 13 per intent, and hide the rest. The baseline uses only those 1,000. Self-training then adopts its own confident predictions, with probability at least 0.8, as pseudo-labels, refits, and repeats.
\end{labquestion}
\begin{labanswer}
\lstinputlisting[style=utppython,firstline=112,lastline=130]{all_experiments/lab05_anomaly_limited_labels/lab05_anomaly_limited_labels_worked.py}
\begin{refoutput}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=utpterminal]
labels only (1,000 messages): validation accuracy 0.638
self-training adopted 486 pseudo-labels, 98.8% of them right
self-training: validation accuracy 0.632
all 8,003 labels (the ceiling): 0.882
\end{lstlisting}
\end{refoutput}

\textbf{What it means.} Self-training did not help: its 486 pseudo-labels were almost all right, yet the score fell slightly. With 77 intents and 13 labels each, the model is confident only about messages it already gets right, so the pseudo-labels teach it nothing new, and they tilt it towards the intents it already knows. The ceiling, 0.882 with every label, shows how much was left on the table. Without the labels-only baseline, ``self-training scored 0.632'' would have sounded like a result; Your-turn task~3 lowers the threshold to see what more pseudo-labels do.
\end{labanswer}

\textbf{Connect it to the chapter.} A score is only useful with a threshold you chose, and a limited label budget is the same kind of decision.

\begin{expectedfindings}
\textbf{Warm-up.} ``Never fraud'' is $1-148/85{,}443=99.83\%$ accurate and catches nothing. A random ranking's average precision equals the prevalence, 0.0017. A random 100 would hold $100\times0.00173\approx0.17$ frauds, so 39 is about 225 times better.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Logistic regression with labels: ROC AUC 0.956, average precision 0.711, 86 frauds in the top 100. The Isolation Forest without labels: 0.950, 0.219 and 39. ROC AUC barely moves, but labels more than double the frauds at the top of the list. When labels exist, use them; the anomaly detector is for when they do not, or for kinds of fraud no label describes yet.
  \item Split at hour 36.9, the Isolation Forest's ROC AUC is still 0.944, but its average precision falls to 0.044 and its top 100 holds just one fraud. Ordinary behavior in the later hours differs from what the forest learned in the earlier ones, so unusual-but-honest transactions crowd the top of the list. The supervised model, which learned what fraud looks like, keeps an average precision of 0.706 and 72 frauds in its top 100. An anomaly detector finds what is unusual, and what is unusual changes over time.
  \item At 0.5 it adopts 3,604 pseudo-labels, 76.8\% of them right, and validation accuracy falls to 0.596; at 0.3 it adopts 5,813, only 69.6\% right, and scores 0.606. Both are worse than the 1,000 labels alone. A lower threshold buys more pseudo-labels, but every wrong one is learned as if it were true, and the next round builds on it: confirmation bias, measured. Always compute the baseline before reporting a semi-supervised method.
  \item The frauds found are 25, 39, 63, 99, 116 and 123, so the net savings are \$4,750, \$7,300, \$11,100, \$14,800, \$13,200 and $-\$400$. A budget of about 1,000 reviews is best at these costs: beyond it, each extra review finds too little fraud to pay for itself. Change the costs, and the answer moves.
\end{enumerate}
\end{expectedfindings}
