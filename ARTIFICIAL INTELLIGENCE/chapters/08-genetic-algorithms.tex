\label{ch:ga}

\chapterguide%

  {name the parts of a genetic algorithm and what each contributes; encode a real-valued problem as a chromosome; implement tournament selection, blend crossover, Gaussian mutation and elitism; evaluate a stochastic optimiser over repeated seeds; and decide when an evolutionary method is justified against an exact one.}

A \term{genetic algorithm} (GA) improves a population of solutions through selection, recombination and random mutation. It needs only a scoring function, not derivatives or convexity. Lab~8 tests a GA on a known one-dimensional function, then fits linear-regression coefficients. Ordinary least squares provides an exact, faster reference: the aim is to evaluate a stochastic optimiser, not to claim it beats an exact method.

The lab uses the same loop for a scalar $x$ and a five-gene vector. The scalar version is:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=aipython]
for _ in range(generations):
    fitness = (population - 3) ** 2                                  # lower is better
    elite = population[np.argsort(fitness)[:n_elite]]
    contestants = rng.integers(0, pop_size, size=(pop_size, 3))      # tournament of three
    parents = population[contestants[np.arange(pop_size), fitness[contestants].argmin(axis=1)]]
    a, b = parents[0::2], parents[1::2]
    alpha = rng.random(len(a))                                       # blend crossover
    children = np.r_[alpha * a + (1 - alpha) * b, alpha * b + (1 - alpha) * a][: pop_size - n_elite]
    children += rng.normal(0, 0.15, len(children))                  # Gaussian mutation
    population = np.r_[elite, children]
\end{lstlisting}

Vectorised operators avoid individual-level loops; 160 generations of 100 chromosomes take a few hundredths of a second.

\section{Encoding: the chromosome is the answer}

For $\hat y=p_0+p_1x_1+\dots+p_4x_4$, encode each chromosome as five real coefficients. The GA searches the same space as least squares, enabling direct comparison. Two details matter:

\begin{description}
  \item[Initialisation matters.] Charges are in thousands of dollars. Draw coefficients with mean~0 and standard deviation~5000, then shift the intercept towards mean charge; $\mathcal N(0,1)$ would start far from useful values.
  \item[Standardise the inputs.] Age (18--64) and smoker status (0--1) have different scales. Standardise all four predictors using training statistics so coefficient mutation steps are comparable.
\end{description}

\begin{intuition}
The chromosome \emph{is} the candidate answer, read by the fitness function. Operators depend on encoding: blend crossover suits real coefficients, not permutations.
\end{intuition}

\section{The operators}

\subsection{Fitness}

Fitness is training mean squared error. The lab evaluates it for the whole population in one matrix product:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=aipython]
def training_mse(individuals):
    predictions = individuals[:, [0]] + individuals[:, 1:] @ X_train.T
    return ((predictions - y_train) ** 2).mean(axis=1)
\end{lstlisting}

Evaluating 100 five-gene chromosomes on 1,070 training rows produces a $100\times1{,}070$ prediction matrix. Vectorisation reduces fitness-evaluation overhead.

\subsection{Selection}

\subsection{Crossover and mutation}

\term{Blend crossover} forms $\alpha\,a+(1-\alpha)\,b$ from two parents. With $\alpha\in[0,1]$, children lie between them; the regression GA uses $\alpha\in[-0.1,1.1]$ to allow extrapolation (\figref{fig:crossover}). \term{Gaussian mutation} adds noise to a random 15\,\% of genes using per-gene step sizes.

\subsection{Elitism}

\term{Elitism} copies the best chromosomes unchanged, preventing selection or mutation from losing them. It reserves population slots and keeps best-so-far fitness from worsening, producing monotone convergence curves.

\begin{keyidea}
Selection favours good candidates, crossover recombines, mutation explores and elitism preserves. Tournament size~3, 15\,\% mutation and four elites are one workable balance, not universal settings.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Verifying before trusting}

Wrong fitness signs or mutation scales may not raise errors. Lab~8 first minimises $f(x)=(x-3)^2$, reaching within $10^{-5}$ of $x=3$ in 80 generations, then checks regression against least squares.

\section{Judging a stochastic optimiser}

One seed shows one outcome. Lab~8 reports results over ten seeds and compares them with an exact method.

Three lessons follow:

\begin{description}
  \item[The GA approaches the optimum.] Runs agree within a few thousandths and match OLS performance; median coefficients match OLS to one decimal place.
  \item[It did not beat the optimum.] Least squares solves the same problem exactly and faster. Agreement verifies the GA, not an advantage over OLS.
  \item[Population size trades speed for spread.] Forty chromosomes halve runtime with the same median, but increase seed-to-seed standard deviation by an order of magnitude. Judge the trade against the cost of a poor run.
\end{description}

\begin{pitfall}
Report the median and spread across independent seeds, not one run or the best run presented as typical.
\end{pitfall}

\section{When is a genetic algorithm the right tool?}

Least squares suits Lab~8\textquotesingle s smooth, convex, unconstrained objective. Consider a GA when the problem instead has:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the objective is \textbf{non-differentiable} or only available as a black box (a simulation, a physical test rig);
  \item some variables are \textbf{discrete or mixed} --- which features to include, which route to take, which schedule to run --- so there is no gradient to follow;
  \item the objective is \textbf{multimodal}, with many local optima that trap gradient methods;
  \item the constraints are \textbf{awkward}: business rules, integer budgets, feasibility checks that are easy to test and impossible to write as equations.
\end{itemize}

Integer coefficients, penalties on non-zero coefficients or a plateaued loss would invalidate ordinary least squares. A GA would need a revised fitness function and possibly new operators; the deliverable asks you to identify one such change.

\begin{keyidea}
Match the optimiser to the structure of the problem. Reach for an exact method when one exists; reach for a genetic algorithm when the objective is a black box, the variables are discrete, or the constraints do not fit an equation --- and then judge it over repeated seeds.
\end{keyidea}
