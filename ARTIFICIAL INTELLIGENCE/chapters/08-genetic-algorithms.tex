\chapter{Genetic Algorithms}
\label{ch:ga}

\chapterguide%
  {Be comfortable with NumPy arrays and random numbers, and know what a regression model and its RMSE are.}
  {name the parts of a genetic algorithm and what each contributes; encode a real-valued problem as a chromosome; implement tournament selection, blend crossover, Gaussian mutation and elitism; evaluate a stochastic optimiser over repeated seeds; and decide when an evolutionary method is justified against an exact one.}

A \term{genetic algorithm} (GA) is an optimiser that keeps a population of candidate solutions and improves them by imitating natural selection: the better candidates are more likely to reproduce, reproduction mixes their parts, and small random changes keep new possibilities arriving. It needs no derivatives, no convexity and no closed form --- only a way to score a candidate. That generality is its appeal and its cost, and Lab~8 is designed to show both. The GA is first verified on a one-dimensional function whose answer is known, then set to fit the coefficients of a linear regression, a problem that ordinary least squares solves exactly in a fraction of a millisecond. The point is not that the GA wins. It is to learn how to run and judge a stochastic optimiser, using a case where the right answer is available to check against.

\section{One generation}

\figref{fig:ga-loop} is the whole algorithm. Each stage has one job, and the population only improves when all three pressures --- selection, recombination, novelty --- act together.

The lab implements the loop twice with the same structure --- once for a scalar $x$, once for a five-gene vector --- so the two versions can be read side by side. In the scalar version:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=aipython]
for _ in range(generations):
    fitness = (population - 3) ** 2                                  # lower is better
    elite = population[np.argsort(fitness)[:n_elite]]
    contestants = rng.integers(0, pop_size, size=(pop_size, 3))      # tournament of three
    parents = population[contestants[np.arange(pop_size), fitness[contestants].argmin(axis=1)]]
    a, b = parents[0::2], parents[1::2]
    alpha = rng.random(len(a))                                       # blend crossover
    children = np.r_[alpha * a + (1 - alpha) * b, alpha * b + (1 - alpha) * a][: pop_size - n_elite]
    children += rng.normal(0, 0.15, len(children))                  # Gaussian mutation
    population = np.r_[elite, children]
\end{lstlisting}

Every operator is one line of array arithmetic; there is no loop over individuals. That is why 160 generations of 100 chromosomes take a few hundredths of a second.

\section{Encoding: the chromosome is the answer}

The first design decision is what a chromosome \emph{is}. For a linear model $\hat y=p_0+p_1x_1+\dots+p_4x_4$ the natural encoding is direct: five real numbers, one per coefficient. The GA then searches the same five-dimensional space that least squares solves algebraically, which is what makes the comparison in Lab~8 clean. Two practical details follow from the encoding.

\begin{description}
  \item[Initialisation matters.] Charges are in the thousands of dollars, so a population drawn from $\mathcal N(0,1)$ would start absurdly far from any good solution. The lab draws coefficients from $\mathcal N(0,5000)$ and shifts the intercept gene to start near the mean charge.
  \item[Standardise the inputs.] One mutation step size is used for every coefficient gene. If \texttt{age} ran 18--64 and \texttt{smoker} 0--1, the same step would be huge for one coefficient and negligible for the other. Standardising the predictors puts all four on the same scale --- with statistics from the training rows only, as in every other lab.
\end{description}

\begin{intuition}
The chromosome is not a description of the answer; it \emph{is} the answer, and the fitness function is what reads it. Change the encoding and every operator has to be reconsidered: blend crossover makes sense for real coefficients and none at all for a permutation.
\end{intuition}

\section{The operators}

\subsection{Fitness}

Fitness is training mean squared error. The lab evaluates it for the whole population in one matrix product:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=aipython]
def training_mse(individuals):
    predictions = individuals[:, [0]] + individuals[:, 1:] @ X_train.T
    return ((predictions - y_train) ** 2).mean(axis=1)
\end{lstlisting}

A $100\times5$ population against $1{,}070$ training rows is a $100\times1{,}070$ prediction matrix; NumPy does it in microseconds. Fitness evaluation is usually the expensive part of a GA, so vectorising it is the single most effective optimisation.

\subsection{Selection}

\term{Tournament selection} picks $k$ chromosomes at random and keeps the fittest; the lab uses $k=3$. It is cheap, needs no scaling of fitness values, and gives every individual a chance while favouring the good ones. The classic alternative, \term{roulette-wheel} selection (\figref{fig:roulette}), makes the probability of being chosen proportional to fitness; it is intuitive but sensitive to the scale of the fitness values and to a single dominant individual, and it must be inverted for minimisation problems. Tournament selection sidesteps all of that.

\subsection{Crossover and mutation}

For real-valued genes the lab uses \term{blend crossover}: each child is a weighted average of its two parents, $\alpha\,a+(1-\alpha)\,b$, with $\alpha$ drawn at random. With $\alpha\in[0,1]$ the child lies on the segment between the parents; the regression GA draws $\alpha\in[-0.1,1.1]$ so that children can land slightly \emph{outside} the segment and the population does not shrink towards its own centre (\figref{fig:crossover}). \term{Gaussian mutation} then adds normal noise to a random 15\,\% of the genes, scaled by a per-gene step size --- the only operator that can produce a value neither parent had.

\subsection{Elitism}

Selection is probabilistic and mutation is blind, so without a safeguard the best chromosome of one generation can be lost in the next. \term{Elitism} copies the best few individuals into the new generation unchanged. It costs a few population slots and guarantees that the best-so-far fitness never gets worse --- which is what makes the convergence curves in Lab~8 monotone.

\begin{keyidea}
Selection exploits, mutation explores, crossover recombines, elitism remembers. Remove any one and the GA either stalls, wanders, or forgets; the settings in the lab (tournament of 3, 15\,\% mutation, 4 elites) are one workable balance, not the only one.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Verifying before trusting}

A GA can run without error and be wrong: a sign flipped in the fitness, a crossover that averages towards zero, a mutation scale so small that nothing moves. Lab~8 therefore starts with $f(x)=(x-3)^2$, whose minimum is known. The scalar GA lands within $10^{-5}$ of $x=3$ in 80 generations. Only then is the same loop applied to the regression problem, where the answer is not known in advance --- or rather, is known through a different method, which is the next section.

\section{Judging a stochastic optimiser}

A GA's output depends on its random seed. One run tells you what happened once; it does not tell you what the algorithm does. Lab~8 therefore runs ten seeds and reports the distribution, and then compares it with an exact method.

\begin{mltable}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\bfseries\leavevmode\color{MLNavy}}p{0.28\linewidth}>{\centering\arraybackslash}X>{\centering\arraybackslash}X>{\centering\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{MLPaleBlue!92}
Method & Median test RMSE & SD across seeds & Median runtime \\
\midrule
GA, population 100 & 5829.38 & 0.002 & 0.04 s \\
GA, population 40 & 5829.38 & 0.026 & 0.02 s \\
Ordinary least squares & 5829.38 & --- & $<$ 0.001 s \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{mltable}

Three observations follow, and each is a general lesson.

\begin{description}
  \item[The GA found the optimum.] Ten seeds agree with each other to a few thousandths and with least squares to the same precision; the GA's median coefficients match the OLS coefficients to one decimal place. On an unconstrained quadratic objective there is nowhere else for a well-tuned GA to go.
  \item[It did not beat the optimum.] Least squares solves the same problem exactly, in closed form, in a fraction of the time. A GA that reaches the OLS answer has confirmed its own correctness, not demonstrated an advantage.
  \item[Population size trades speed for spread.] Forty chromosomes reach the same median in half the time, but the standard deviation across seeds grows by an order of magnitude. A smaller population has less diversity and depends more on where it started. Whether that trade is acceptable depends on how much a single bad run would cost.
\end{description}

\begin{pitfall}
Never report a GA result from one seed, and never report the \emph{best} of several seeds as if it were typical. The median and the spread over independent runs are the result; the best run is a lucky draw.
\end{pitfall}

\section{When is a genetic algorithm the right tool?}

Least squares wins Lab~8 because the problem is smooth, convex and unconstrained --- exactly the conditions under which exact methods exist. A GA earns its cost when those conditions fail:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the objective is \textbf{non-differentiable} or only available as a black box (a simulation, a physical test rig);
  \item some variables are \textbf{discrete or mixed} --- which features to include, which route to take, which schedule to run --- so there is no gradient to follow;
  \item the objective is \textbf{multimodal}, with many local optima that trap gradient methods;
  \item the constraints are \textbf{awkward}: business rules, integer budgets, feasibility checks that are easy to test and impossible to write as equations.
\end{itemize}

Change Lab~8's problem in any of these directions --- require the coefficients to be integers, penalise the number of non-zero coefficients, replace squared error with a loss that has plateaus --- and least squares no longer applies while the GA needs only a new fitness function and, sometimes, a new mutation operator. The lab's deliverable asks you to name one such change and the operator it would touch.

\begin{keyidea}
Match the optimiser to the structure of the problem. Reach for an exact method when one exists; reach for a genetic algorithm when the objective is a black box, the variables are discrete, or the constraints do not fit an equation --- and then judge it over repeated seeds.
\end{keyidea}
