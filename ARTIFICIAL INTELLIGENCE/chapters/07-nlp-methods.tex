Lab~7 builds a classifier that labels a tweet about an airline as negative, neutral or positive. The model at its centre --- Multinomial Naive Bayes over word counts --- is one of the oldest in the field and fits in a few lines. The lab is not really about the model. It is about the protocol around it: how to prepare text without cheating, how to choose settings without touching the test data, which numbers to report when two thirds of the tweets belong to one class, and how to look at the mistakes afterwards. That protocol transfers unchanged to models a thousand times larger.

\section{From text to numbers}

A classifier needs a fixed-length numeric input. The simplest representation of a document is the \term{bag of words}: a vector with one position per vocabulary term holding how often that term occurs, with word order thrown away. Building it is a pipeline of its own (\figref{fig:preprocess}).

Lab~7's normaliser does two things beyond lowercasing. It replaces every URL with the token \texttt{URL} and every \texttt{@handle} with \texttt{USER}, because the specific address or account carries no sentiment and would otherwise create thousands of one-off vocabulary terms. \texttt{CountVectorizer} then tokenises, drops English stopwords, keeps only terms that appear in at least two training tweets, and caps the vocabulary at 20,000.

\subsection{N-grams}

A bag of single words cannot see that ``never again'' is a phrase. Adding \term{bigrams} --- every adjacent pair of tokens --- gives the model such phrases as extra terms. In the lab, unigrams alone produce 4,671 features and unigrams plus bigrams 11,850. The cost is a sparser matrix and more parameters; whether it is worth paying is a question for the validation set, not for intuition.

\section{Multinomial Naive Bayes}

Naive Bayes chooses the class $c$ that maximises the posterior probability of the document $d$:
\[
\hat c=\argmax_c\; P(c)\prod_{w\in d}P(w\mid c)^{n_w},
\]
where $n_w$ is the count of term $w$ in the document. $P(c)$ is the class's share of the training data; $P(w\mid c)$ is how often $w$ appears among all the words of class-$c$ training documents. ``Naive'' names the assumption that terms are independent given the class, which is false for language and works anyway. \figref{fig:naive-bayes} shows the arithmetic on one sentence.

\subsection{Smoothing}

A term that never appears in the positive training tweets has $P(w\mid\text{positive})=0$, and one zero factor annihilates the whole product no matter what the other words say. \term{Laplace smoothing} adds a pseudo-count $\alpha$ to every term in every class:
\[
P(w\mid c)=\frac{\text{count}(w,c)+\alpha}{\sum_{w'}\text{count}(w',c)+\alpha\,|V|}.
\]
Small $\alpha$ trusts the counts; large $\alpha$ pulls every probability towards uniform. In practice the products are computed as sums of logarithms to avoid underflow, and $\alpha$ is a hyperparameter to be chosen on validation data. Lab~7 tries $\alpha\in\{0.3,0.5,1,2\}$ and finds $0.3$ best, with the n-gram choice mattering more than the smoothing.

\begin{intuition}
Naive Bayes is a vote in which every word in the tweet casts a weighted ballot for each class, and the weights are how much more often that word appeared in one class's training tweets than another's. Smoothing makes sure no single unseen word can veto the vote.
\end{intuition}

\section{Metrics for imbalanced classes}

In the lab data 63\,\% of tweets are negative, 21\,\% neutral and 16\,\% positive. A classifier that answers ``negative'' to everything scores 63\,\% accuracy while learning nothing, so accuracy cannot be the headline number. Per class, three quantities matter:

\begin{mltable}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\bfseries\leavevmode\color{MLNavy}}p{0.16\linewidth}p{0.36\linewidth}X@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{MLPaleBlue!92}
Metric & Definition & Answers \\
\midrule
Precision & Of the tweets predicted as class $c$, the fraction that are $c$ & When the model says positive, how often is it right? \\
Recall & Of the tweets that are $c$, the fraction predicted as $c$ & How many of the positive tweets did it find? \\
F1 & Harmonic mean of precision and recall & One number per class that punishes neglecting either \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{mltable}

\term{Macro F1} averages the three class F1 scores with equal weight, so the 16\,\% positive class counts as much as the 63\,\% negative one. The majority baseline has recall~1 on negative and recall~0 on the other two, giving macro F1 of $0.26$; that is the number a real model must beat. Naive Bayes reaches $0.70$.

\begin{pitfall}
Always compute the majority-class baseline before training anything. Without it, ``77\,\% accuracy'' sounds like an achievement; next to a baseline of 63\,\% it is a modest gain, and next to macro F1 of $0.26$ versus $0.70$ it is a large one. The baseline is what makes the comparison honest.
\end{pitfall}

\section{A leakage-safe protocol}

\term{Leakage} is any way that information from the test data reaches the model or the choices made about it. Text classification has more of these leaks than most tasks, and Lab~7 closes each one in a specific place (\figref{fig:protocol}).

\begin{aifig}[The evaluation protocol of Lab~7]{Where each decision is made and what data it may see. Duplicates are removed before the split so no near-copy can straddle it; the vectoriser and the model are fitted on training tweets only; every hyperparameter choice uses the validation set; the test set is read once, at the end.}{fig:protocol}

\node[s] (dedup) at (0,0) {\textbf{1. Deduplicate}\\exact and normalised\\copies removed};

\node[s,right=0.45cm of split,fill=MLPaleOrange,draw=MLOrange] (fit) {\textbf{3. Fit on train}\\vocabulary and\\Naive Bayes};
\node[s,right=0.45cm of fit,fill=MLPaleOrange,draw=MLOrange] (tune) {\textbf{4. Tune on validation}\\n-grams and $\alpha$\\by macro F1};

\begin{description}
  \item[Duplicates before the split.] 213 tweets are exact copies of another and 355 become identical after URLs and mentions are replaced. A retweet in the training set with its twin in the test set would be a memorised answer. The lab keeps one copy of each, \emph{then} splits.
  \item[Stratified, seeded, saved.] The 70/15/15 split preserves the class mix in every subset, uses a fixed seed so it can be reproduced, and writes the assignment to a file so it can be audited.
  \item[Vocabulary from training text only.] \texttt{CountVectorizer} is fitted on training tweets and merely applied to the rest. A word that occurs only in test tweets is unknown to the model --- which is exactly the situation a deployed classifier faces.
  \item[Selection on validation, one look at test.] Eight settings are scored on the validation set; the best is refitted and scored on the test set exactly once. The test score is then an estimate of performance on new tweets, not the best of eight draws.
\end{description}

\begin{keyidea}
The order of operations is the method. Deduplicate, split, fit, tune, test --- each step may only see the data the previous steps left it. Reversing any two adjacent steps leaks information forward, and the leak shows up as a test score that is too good to be true.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Reading the evidence}

The lab produces four kinds of evidence, each answering a question the headline score cannot.

\begin{description}
  \item[The confusion matrix.] A $3\times3$ table of true class against predicted class. For the lab's model the off-diagonal mass sits between neutral and negative: neutral tweets are pulled towards negative, because complaint vocabulary dominates the corpus and a neutral question about a delay contains the word ``delay''.
  \item[Per-class scores.] Negative F1 $0.86$, positive $0.67$, neutral $0.56$. Neutral is the hardest class: it has the fewest distinctive words and sits between the other two.
  \item[Per-group scores.] Macro F1 computed separately for each airline ranges from $0.66$ to $0.78$. This is a diagnostic, not a league table: the airlines differ in how many test tweets they have (63 to 594), in their class mix and in what customers write about. A subgroup breakdown shows where the model is weak; it does not rank the subjects.
  \item[An error sample.] The four most confident mistakes for each true class --- twelve tweets, chosen by a rule so that the sample is reproducible --- tagged with a simple category. In the lab's sample the categories are negations the bag of words cannot see, questions with little sentiment vocabulary, very short tweets, and one joke retweeted many times and labelled differently by different annotators. The last of these is a label-quality finding, not a model finding, and only an error sample could have surfaced it.
\end{description}

\begin{pitfall}
Confidence is not correctness. Every tweet in the error sample was misclassified with a posterior above $0.999$. Naive Bayes multiplies many small probabilities, so its posteriors are extreme by construction; treat them as a ranking, never as a calibrated chance of being right.
\end{pitfall}

\section{What the protocol does not fix}

A bag of words with Naive Bayes has known limits, and the error sample shows them: negation, sarcasm, and any meaning carried by word order. Better representations --- weighted counts, word embeddings, contextual language models --- address these, and the lab's deliverable asks what you would try next. The protocol is what stays fixed when the model changes: the same deduplicated split, the same validation-based selection, the same single look at the test set. A stronger model evaluated with a weaker protocol is not an improvement; it is a number nobody can trust.
