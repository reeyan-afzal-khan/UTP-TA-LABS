\term{Natural Language Processing (NLP)} applies linguistics, computer science, and machine learning to analyze or generate text and speech.

\section{Why NLP is needed}

NLP converts unstructured language into representations suitable for interaction, automation, and information extraction.

Applications include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item healthcare: analyzing patient notes and assisting diagnosis;
  \item business: chatbots, sentiment analysis, and automated email sorting;
  \item education: grammar correction and automated essay scoring;
  \item law: legal-document summarization and contract analysis;
  \item robotics: verbal commands and real-time interaction.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keyidea}
Each example follows a pipeline: input, representation, and task-specific output.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Text similarity with word embeddings}

The first MATLAB example compares
\begin{quote}
``The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog.''
\end{quote}
with
\begin{quote}
``A fast brown animal leaps above a sleeping dog.''
\end{quote}
using pretrained FastText word embeddings.

The pipeline has five stages.

\begin{mlsteps}
  \item Tokenize both sentences.
  \item Load pretrained FastText embeddings.
  \item Remove words not available in the embedding vocabulary.
  \item Convert each word to a vector and average the word vectors for each sentence.
  \item Compute cosine similarity between the two sentence vectors.
\end{mlsteps}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=mlmatlab]
clear; clc;

text1 = "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog.";
text2 = "A fast brown animal leaps above a sleeping dog.";

documents = tokenizedDocument([text1, text2]);
emb = fastTextWordEmbedding;

words1 = string(documents(1).Vocabulary);
words2 = string(documents(2).Vocabulary);
words1 = words1(isVocabularyWord(emb, words1));
words2 = words2(isVocabularyWord(emb, words2));

if isempty(words1) || isempty(words2)
    error("A sentence has no words in the embedding vocabulary.");
end

vecs1 = word2vec(emb, words1);
vecs2 = word2vec(emb, words2);
vec1 = mean(vecs1, 1);
vec2 = mean(vecs2, 1);

similarity = dot(vec1, vec2) / (norm(vec1) * norm(vec2));
fprintf("Cosine Similarity between the two texts: %.4f\n", similarity);
\end{lstlisting}

The \texttt{Vocabulary} property contains unique word types, so repeated words do not receive extra weight in this version. A token-level sentence representation would preserve repetition. This distinction matters when word frequency carries meaning.

\section{Cosine similarity}

For sentence vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\), the code computes
\[
\cos(\theta)=\frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}
{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}.
\]
A value near 1 indicates high directional similarity.

\begin{intuition}
A word embedding replaces a word with a numerical vector. Averaging the word vectors gives one rough vector for a sentence. Cosine similarity then asks whether the two sentence vectors point in similar directions.
\end{intuition}

\begin{mlfigure}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1.2cm,y=1.2cm]
  \draw[-{Stealth[length=2mm]},draw=MLMuted] (-0.15,0)--(4.2,0) node[right,font=\scriptsize] {dimension 1};
  \draw[-{Stealth[length=2mm]},draw=MLMuted] (0,-0.15)--(0,2.7) node[above,font=\scriptsize] {dimension 2};
  \draw[-{Stealth[length=2.5mm]},very thick,draw=MLBlue] (0,0)--(3.4,1.15)
    node[right,font=\small\bfseries\color{MLBlue}] {$\mathbf{u}$};
  \draw[-{Stealth[length=2.5mm]},very thick,draw=MLTeal] (0,0)--(2.8,1.75)
    node[above right,font=\small\bfseries\color{MLTeal}] {$\mathbf{v}$};
  \draw[-{Stealth[length=1.5mm]},draw=MLOrange,thick]
    (0.92,0.31) arc[start angle=18.7,end angle=32,radius=0.97];
  \node[font=\scriptsize\bfseries\color{MLOrange!85!black}] at (1.15,0.55) {$\theta$};
  \node[align=left,font=\scriptsize\color{MLMuted}] at (3.45,2.35)
    {small angle $\Rightarrow$\\high cosine similarity};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{mlfigure}

Cosine similarity compares direction, not magnitude. Two sentences can have vectors of different lengths and still be judged similar when the vectors point in nearly the same direction. A vector pointing in the opposite direction has a negative cosine, while perpendicular vectors have cosine zero.

\begin{workedexample}
For a small numerical check, let \(\mathbf{u}=(1,1)\) and \(\mathbf{v}=(2,1)\). Their dot product is 3, their norms are \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(\sqrt{5}\), and their cosine similarity is
\[
\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{5}}=\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\approx0.949.
\]
The value is high because the vectors point in similar directions; MATLAB applies the same calculation in higher dimensions.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Image captioning using AlexNet: a classification-based simulation}

The second Lab 6 example loads an image, resizes it to AlexNet's expected input size, extracts \texttt{fc7} features, classifies the image, and turns the predicted class label into a short caption.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=mlmatlab]
clear; clc;
img = imread('plane.jpg');
imshow(img);
title('Input Image');

net = alexnet;
imgResized = imresize(img, net.Layers(1).InputSize(1:2));

featureLayer = 'fc7';
features = activations(net, imgResized, featureLayer, ...
    'OutputAs', 'rows');

[label, scores] = classify(net, imgResized);
disp(['Predicted Object: ', char(label)]);

caption = "This is a picture of a " + lower(char(label)) + ".";
disp("Generated Caption:");
disp(caption);
\end{lstlisting}

The \texttt{features} variable demonstrates how an intermediate neural representation can be extracted, but the fixed-template caption does not use it directly. The \texttt{classify} call produces the label inserted into the caption.

\begin{pitfall}
This is classification with a fixed caption template, not free-form image captioning.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Scene text detection and translation}

The third Lab 6 pipeline uses optical character recognition (OCR) to extract printed text from an image and a hard-coded English-to-French dictionary to simulate translation without an internet application programming interface (API).

\begin{mlfigure}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
  b/.style={draw=MLBlue,fill=MLPaleBlue,rounded corners=2mm,minimum width=2.45cm,minimum height=0.9cm,align=center,font=\small\color{MLNavy}},
  arr/.style={-{Stealth[length=2mm]},thick,draw=MLTeal}
]
\node[b] (img) {Scene image};
\node[b,right=0.30cm of img] (ocr) {OCR\\\scriptsize detected text};
\node[b,right=0.30cm of ocr] (norm) {Normalize\\\scriptsize uppercase / trim};
\node[b,right=0.30cm of norm] (dict) {Dictionary lookup};
\node[b,right=0.30cm of dict] (out) {Translated text};
\draw[arr] (img)--(ocr); \draw[arr] (ocr)--(norm); \draw[arr] (norm)--(dict); \draw[arr] (dict)--(out);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{mlfigure}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=mlmatlab]
img = imread('street.jpg');
imshow(img);
title("Scene Text Image");

ocrResult = ocr(img);
detectedText = upper(strtrim(ocrResult.Text));
disp("Detected Text:");
disp(detectedText);

dictionary = containers.Map;
dictionary("STOP") = "ARRET";
dictionary("SPEED LIMIT") = "LIMITATION DE VITESSE";
dictionary("YIELD") = "CEDEZ LE PASSAGE";
dictionary("NO PARKING") = "STATIONNEMENT INTERDIT";
dictionary("ONE WAY") = "SENS UNIQUE";
dictionary("DO NOT ENTER") = "ENTREE INTERDITE";
dictionary("STREET") = "RUE";

% Try longer phrases first so that a specific phrase wins.
sourcePhrases = string(keys(dictionary));
[~, order] = sort(strlength(sourcePhrases), 'descend');
sourcePhrases = sourcePhrases(order);
translatedText = "";

for i = 1:numel(sourcePhrases)
    phrase = char(sourcePhrases(i));
    if contains(detectedText, string(phrase))
        translatedText = string(dictionary(phrase));
        break;
    end
end

if translatedText == ""
    translatedText = "No dictionary translation found";
end
disp("French translation:");
disp(translatedText);
\end{lstlisting}

The \texttt{contains} check tolerates surrounding OCR text, but translation remains limited to stored dictionary phrases.

\begin{workedexample}
\texttt{"STOP\textbackslash nAHEAD"} still matches \texttt{STOP} and returns \texttt{ARRET}; \texttt{S70P} requires partial matching or a clearer image.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Extensions in the OCR examples}

Lab 6 gives three additional variations.

\begin{description}
  \item[Multi-language dictionary] A STOP sign is mapped independently to French and German dictionaries.
  \item[Fuzzy keyword matching] Partial keywords such as \texttt{PARK} or \texttt{SPEED} trigger translations even when the OCR text is not an exact dictionary key.
  \item[Region-of-interest (ROI) selection] The user draws a rectangle around the relevant image region before OCR, reducing the text-recognition area.
\end{description}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=mlmatlab]
img = imread('stop.jpg');
imshow(img); title("Select Region for OCR");
roi = drawrectangle;
pause(1);
ocrRes = ocr(img, roi.Position);
text = upper(strtrim(ocrRes.Text));
disp("Detected Text: " + text);
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{quickcheck}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What role do pretrained FastText vectors play in the similarity example?
  \item Why are the word vectors averaged before cosine similarity is computed?
  \item What produces the caption text in the AlexNet example?
  \item What is the difference between OCR and the dictionary translation step?
  \item Why can selecting a region of interest change the OCR result?
\end{enumerate}
\end{quickcheck}

\begin{checkanswers}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item FastText maps recognized tokens to semantic vectors learned from a large text corpus.
  \item Averaging creates one fixed-length sentence representation from a variable number of word vectors.
  \item A fixed sentence template inserts AlexNet's predicted class label.
  \item OCR recognizes characters in pixels; the dictionary maps recognized phrases to stored translations.
  \item A region of interest removes irrelevant pixels and can make the target text larger and less ambiguous to OCR.
\end{enumerate}
\end{checkanswers}
