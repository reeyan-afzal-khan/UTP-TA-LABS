\chapter{CPU Scheduling}\label{ch:scheduling}
\chapterguide{Know the process-state model from Chapter~\ref{ch:overview}.}{Compute waiting, turnaround, response, and completion times; trace FCFS, SJF, Priority and Round Robin; and explain fairness and preemption trade-offs.}

\section{Why scheduling exists}
When more runnable processes exist than available CPU cores, the OS must decide which ready process executes next. The \term{scheduler} applies a policy; the \term{dispatcher} performs the context switch and transfers control.

\section{Core timing quantities}
For process $i$:
\[
T_i = C_i - A_i, \qquad W_i = T_i - B_i, \qquad R_i = S_i - A_i,
\]
where $A_i$ is arrival time, $B_i$ burst/service time, $S_i$ first start time, $C_i$ completion time, $T_i$ turnaround time, $W_i$ waiting time, and $R_i$ response time.

\begin{workedexample}
If a process arrives at time 2, first runs at 5, needs 4 CPU units in total, and finishes at time 11, then $R=5-2=3$, $T=11-2=9$, and $W=9-4=5$.
\end{workedexample}

\section{First-Come, First-Served}
\term{FCFS} is non-preemptive: ready processes run in arrival order. It is simple, but a long CPU-bound job can delay many short jobs, producing the \term{convoy effect}.

\section{Shortest Job First}
\term{SJF} chooses the ready process with the smallest predicted CPU burst. In the idealized non-preemptive case, it minimizes average waiting time for a known set of bursts. The practical problem is that future CPU bursts are not known exactly.

\section{Priority scheduling}
Priority scheduling selects the highest-priority ready process. A convention must be stated: in many exercises a smaller numeric value means a higher priority. Long-term starvation of low-priority processes can be reduced through \term{aging}, which gradually improves the priority of waiting jobs.

\section{Round Robin}
\term{Round Robin (RR)} is preemptive. Each ready process receives at most a \term{time quantum} $q$, then unfinished processes move to the back of the ready queue.

\begin{mltable}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\bfseries\color{MLNavy}}p{0.17\linewidth}p{0.19\linewidth}XX@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{MLPaleBlue!92}
Policy & Preemptive? & Main advantage & Main risk / cost \\
\midrule
FCFS & No & Very simple and low overhead & Convoy effect \\
SJF & Usually taught non-preemptive first & Low average waiting with known bursts & Burst estimate required; starvation possible \\
Priority & Either & Expresses importance/urgency & Starvation without aging \\
Round Robin & Yes & Responsive and fair for time sharing & Context-switch overhead if $q$ is too small \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{mltable}

\begin{intuition}
The time quantum is a policy knob. A very large $q$ makes Round Robin resemble FCFS. A very small $q$ improves responsiveness but spends more CPU time on context switching.
\end{intuition}

\section{Gantt charts are state traces}
A Gantt chart should show which process owns the CPU over each interval. From that trace, calculate completion times first; then derive turnaround and waiting times using the equations above.

Processes P1, P2, P3 all arrive at time 0 with bursts 5, 3, and 1. Draw schedules for FCFS, SJF, and RR with $q=2$. Compare average waiting time and the time each process first receives the CPU.
\end{tryit}

\begin{chapterrecap}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Scheduling selects a ready process when CPU demand exceeds available processors.
  \item Waiting, turnaround, and response time measure different user-visible effects.
  \item FCFS is simple; SJF favors short work; priority expresses importance; RR emphasizes time-sharing fairness.
  \item Every scheduling result depends on assumptions such as arrival times, preemption, and tie-breaking.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterrecap}
