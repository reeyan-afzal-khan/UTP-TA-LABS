\labsection{4}{CPU scheduling: compare policies on one workload}{sec:lab04}
\begin{labproject}{Use hand traces and small simulators to compare FCFS, SJF, Priority, and Round Robin}
\textbf{Suggested time:} 110--120 minutes \hfill
\textbf{Level:} intermediate\par
\textbf{Starter files:} \texttt{fcfs.c}, \texttt{sjf.c}, \texttt{priority.c}, \texttt{round\_robin.c}.\par
\textbf{Evidence to keep:} Gantt charts, timing table, one quantum comparison, and one interpretation paragraph.

\begin{labmode}
The code is a checker for your reasoning, not a substitute for it. Keep the workload constant while comparing policies so the scheduling rule is the only deliberate change.
\end{labmode}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Learning outcomes.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item calculate completion, turnaround, waiting, and response time;
  \item distinguish non-preemptive selection from Round Robin time slicing;
  \item explain a scheduling trade-off without declaring one policy ``best'' for every workload.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Common workload.}
All processes arrive at time 0. Burst times are P1=8, P2=4, P3=2, P4=1. Priorities are 3, 1, 4, 2 respectively, where a smaller number is higher priority. Start Round Robin with quantum 2.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Pre-lab trace.}
Before compiling anything, draw the FCFS Gantt chart and calculate each process's waiting and turnaround time. Check the identity
\[
\text{turnaround} = \text{completion} - \text{arrival},\qquad
\text{waiting} = \text{turnaround} - \text{burst}.
\]

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part A --- FCFS establishes the baseline.}
Compile and run \texttt{fcfs.c}. Compare every value with your hand trace. Then reverse the burst order in the source and rerun. Explain why total CPU work is unchanged while average waiting time can change.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part B --- SJF changes the selection rule.}
Run \texttt{sjf.c} on the original workload. Draw its Gantt chart and compare average waiting time with FCFS. State the information SJF needs that a real scheduler cannot know perfectly in advance.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part C --- Priority requires a convention.}
Run \texttt{priority.c}. Record the priority convention. Give P1 and P2 equal priority, rerun, and identify the tie-break rule implemented by the starter program.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part D --- Round Robin makes response visible.}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=osbash]
gcc -Wall -Wextra round_robin.c -o round_robin
./round_robin 2
./round_robin 1
./round_robin 8
\end{lstlisting}
For quantum 2, draw every slice. Record each process's first response time, waiting time, and turnaround time. Then compare only these three quantum choices.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part E --- Interpret, do not just rank.}
Write 5--7 sentences comparing the policies for this workload. Mention at least two metrics and one limitation or assumption. Avoid a blanket statement such as ``SJF is the best scheduler.''

\begin{verification}
A complete lab record contains four labelled Gantt charts, a timing table, the quantum 1/2/8 comparison, and calculations that can be checked independently from the program output.
\end{verification}

\begin{tryit}
Optional extension: introduce a non-zero arrival time for one process on paper. Explain why simply sorting all jobs once at time 0 no longer represents a correct scheduler.
\end{tryit}
\end{labproject}
